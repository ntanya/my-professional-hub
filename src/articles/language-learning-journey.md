To set the scene: my native language is Russian.

## How everything started

My English-learning journey started with blood, sweat and tears in my first grade, when our drop-dead-gorgeous teacher, a short-haired brunette with lips glossed in shiny pink, gave an assignment: make flash cards for the words that we learned in the classroom.

I had poor drawing skills at that age, and drove my grandmother crazy with demands to make sketches of a pen, a pencil, a dog and an apple, on the 3''x3'' cutout white paper squares, so I could color them.

After that emotional craft project, the first English words got forever imprinted into my brain, the teacher was impressed, and I became the best student in the class and carried that crown all the way through the end of high school.

Ever since then, I can't quit learning languages.

My current obsession is French. And learning French feels like _"une liaison passionnée"_, a tumultuous affair with a capricious (albeit super fun and good-looking) lover, whereas English is your trusted, supportive, fully understood and reliable long-term partner, with whom you never have any fights.

This affair has been going on for several years, and although I can speak and feel completely at ease on my annual escapes in Paris, I still haven't wrangled _"mon petit ami français"_ into complete obedience.

This is a long intro to what I actually wanted to share: here is what I tried in language learning, what failed and what finally worked.

## What I tried

- Duolingo and apps
- Group classes in person
- Group classes online
- Individual classes (inconsistent and not systematized)
- Self-learning online tutorials
- Lingoda: self-selected group classes with teachers online
- iTalki: conversation practice with native speakers
- Podcasts, videos, movies

Until finally, for the past year, I've been learning 1-1 with a great teacher and get amazing results.

If you also struggle with languages, it is likely because of fragmented or inefficient use of tools and approaches.

## What failed, and why

**Duolingo and similar apps:** It's such a low-barrier (and addictively gamified) way to get started, click on words and get a false sense of "learning". Apps could be useful with basic concepts and memorization, but they don't really help much with other parts. Well, I guess you can also practice talking to their AI bot, if you want.

**Group classes** are great for beginners, since in a group you're likely forced to repeat the same thing million times over. At some point, group classes stopped working for me because they either cater to students who are at the low edge of the level and it gets boring, or you have some advanced people and they raise their hand and shout all answers, while the rest of the group silently struggles along.

**Online tutorials and platforms like Lingoda** might work, however, you're likely are not using them consistently and they fail to identify gaps in knowledge.

**Online conversation practice with native speakers** — I've tried that too. It certainly helps, but highly depends on who you get on the other side. I've had some jarring experiences with an iron lady who looked at me sternly in silence when I couldn't form a sentence and an old guy who just talked to himself, while I nodded (off).

**Podcasts, books, videos, movies** are great to supplement learning, so definitely use them, because they are fun.

## What finally worked

Now, to the magic person that I can't wait to tell you about!

His name is Thomas.

I found Thomas randomly online and scheduled a trial lesson. And since I've tried everything under the sun to get better in French, I could tell right away that I wanted to learn with him.

Here's why: **he has a system.**

Even in our very first class, we covered everything: reading, speaking, writing, listening comprehension, grammar and vocabulary. Each of those areas have to be practiced and strengthened, otherwise you hobble along like a three-legged dog who can't make any progress.

Writing was my least favorite thing when we started — you don't practice writing if you self-learn or learn in a group class. We also found a ton of gaps in grammar, so we started reviewing it from lower levels and re-paved our way up to B2.

And of course, the 1-1 format is perfect since it's paced and tailored to your exact needs. We typically start with chatting about our week, then review homework (I ask for a ton of homework), then move on to the topic at hand — they always vary which is fun.

Lastly, even with individual classes, you gotta have certain chemistry and enjoy your teacher. I also love Thomas because of the random personal things that he shares here and there during our classes: fun bits about his family, his weekend baking classes where he teaches how make crème brûlée (in French), and the fact that he dreams of being an honorary elected member of [Académie Française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acad%C3%A9mie_Fran%C3%A7aise), which is THE ultimate French language guardian authority.

My affair with French is going to continue until I find my next passion. And I can already feel it: it's going to be Korean and I know exactly how to conquer him very quickly.
